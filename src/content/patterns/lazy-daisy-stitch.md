## Summary

This pattern is for anyone stitching small petals and leaves by hand. It describes the lazy daisy, also called the detached chain stitch: a single loop held down by a tiny stitch at its tip, and how to make each loop plump and evenly placed.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Mark the flower centre and each petal tip with a dot before you start. | Even spacing is the hardest part, and dots take the guesswork out of it. |
| Go down right beside where you came up, a fabric thread away, while holding the loop. | The loop can't slip back through the fabric, and the base of the petal stays narrow. |
| Pull the loop just snug around the needle, not tight. | A gentle pull gives a rounded petal; a hard pull makes it thin and stretched. |
| Keep the tie-down stitch tiny, about 1 mm, and take it just over the loop. | A short anchor holds the tip without distorting it; a longer one adds a stalk. |
| Work opposite petals first, then fill in between. | Balancing the flower early stops petals drifting to one side. |

## Context

This pattern suits daisies, simple five-petal flowers, leaves along a [Stem stitch](../stem-stitch/) vine, scattered fills and children's or folk-style designs. It's one of the quickest ways to add colour.

It does not suit large petals, where a long loop gets floppy and snags, or realistic shading, which suits [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/) or long-and-short stitch.

There's a trade-off in where the needle goes back down. Some teachers go down in the exact same hole the thread came up, which gives the narrowest base, while others go down a fabric thread away so the loop can't pull through. This pattern goes down beside the hole because it's more forgiving for beginners; on firm, tightly woven fabric the same hole works fine.

## Usage

**Marking:** with a removable pen (see [Transferring a design to fabric](../transferring-designs/)), mark a centre point and five petal tips evenly around it, each about 5 to 8 mm from the centre. Hoop the fabric firmly (see [Hooping fabric drum-tight](../hooping-fabric/)). Use two or three strands of stranded cotton, the six-strand floss sold in skeins.

**Making a petal:** bring the needle up at the base of the petal, just outside the centre point. Take it back down right beside that point, a fabric thread away, and pull gently until a loop the size of the petal is left on the surface; hold the loop with your thumb so it doesn't disappear. Bring the needle up at the petal tip, inside the loop. Pull the thread through slowly until the loop closes gently around the working thread; stop when it lies flat and rounded. Then take the needle down just outside the loop, about 1 mm beyond the tip, to make a tiny tie-down stitch.

**Building the flower:** work the first petal, then the one opposite it, then fill in the rest, so the flower stays balanced. Travel to the next petal base on the back; these short jumps are hidden under the petals. Finish the centre with one or more [French knots](../french-knot/).

**Leaves and variations:** for a leaf, make the tie-down stitch longer, about 3 to 5 mm, so it forms a pointed tip or a short stalk. Loops placed in a line make a decorative border, and scattered single loops make a light, textured fill. For a fuller petal, work a second, smaller lazy daisy inside the first in a contrasting colour.
