## Summary

This pattern is for anyone giving a short, timed presentation at a conference, seminar or meeting. It describes how to shape and rehearse a talk so it fits its slot with room to spare.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Decide your one main message before making slides. | A short talk can carry one idea well, and everything else should support it or go. |
| Treat about one slide per minute as a ceiling, not a target. | More slides than minutes almost always means rushing, and some slides need longer than a minute. |
| Rehearse aloud with a timer at least three times. | Talking through it in your head is much faster than speaking, so only aloud rehearsal gives a real time. |
| Cut until your rehearsal takes about 90% of the slot. | Live talks tend to run longer than rehearsals, because of nerves, technical hiccups and pauses for the audience. |
| Mark one or two slides you can drop if you're running late. | You can recover time calmly instead of speeding up or skipping your conclusion. |
| Check the room, projector and clicker before your session. | Connection and display problems are common and much easier to fix during a break than in front of an audience. |
| Bring a backup copy of your slides in a second format. | If your laptop or file fails, a copy on a USB stick or in cloud storage, plus a PDF version, keeps you going. |

## Context

This pattern suits conference talks, lightning talks, short seminar slots and project updates where time is fixed and shared with other speakers.

It does not suit a long lecture or workshop, where pacing is more flexible, or an informal discussion with no slides. For a meeting where you have an agenda item rather than a talk, see [No agenda, no meeting](../meeting-agenda/).

There's a trade-off between content and clarity. Including every result feels thorough, but the audience remembers less. This pattern favours one message delivered clearly. If your audience will read a paper or report afterwards, point them to it for the detail.

## Usage

**Shaping:** write your main message in one sentence. Sketch a beginning (why it matters), a middle (the evidence or example) and an end (the message again, and what you'd like the audience to do).

**Rehearsing:** run the whole talk aloud with a timer, ideally once in front of a colleague. Note where you run long and cut there. Repeat until you're consistently at about 90% of the slot.

**On the day:** check the room in an earlier break and load your slides. Plan the session around your slot using [Plan your conference, leave gaps](../conference-session-plan/). Keep a watch or timer where you can see it, and if you're behind at the halfway mark, drop your marked slides.

## References

Conference organisers usually send speaker instructions with slot lengths and technical details. Many universities and professional associations also publish presentation-skills guides.
