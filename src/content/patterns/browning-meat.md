## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks meat in a pan. It describes how to get a deep brown crust instead of grey, steamed meat, and how to keep the browned bits in the pan as flavour for the rest of the dish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pat the meat dry, then put it in a hot, heavy pan with a thin film of oil. | Surface water has to boil off before meat can brown, and a heavy pan holds its heat when the meat goes in. |
| Don't crowd the pan: leave gaps between pieces and brown in batches. | Too much meat at once cools the pan and releases juices that can't evaporate, so the meat stews. |
| Leave it alone until it releases from the pan, then turn it. | The crust needs uninterrupted contact, and meat that has browned underneath lets go by itself. |
| Keep the brown bits stuck to the pan and lift them with a splash of liquid. | This fond is concentrated flavour, and deglazing turns it into the base of a sauce or stew. |
| Brown for flavour, then cook to a safe temperature. | A browned outside says nothing about the centre, and mince or chicken must be cooked through. |

## Context

This pattern suits a steak or chops cooked in a frying pan, chicken thighs before a braise, beef chunks for a stew or curry, and mince for tacos, burgers or a cottage pie.

It does not suit delicate fish, thin schnitzels that cook through in a minute, or meat you're poaching or steaming on purpose. Very lean mince also browns poorly and dries out if you push it too far.

There's a trade-off between colour and time. Browning in batches takes longer and makes more splatter, and it's tempting to tip everything in at once. For a quick weeknight mince, a lighter brown may be enough. For a stew or a pan sauce, the extra ten minutes is where most of the flavour comes from.

## Usage

**The pan and the heat:** use stainless steel or cast iron if you have it (see [Caring for cast iron](../cast-iron-care/)). They build up fond better than non-stick, which works but browns less and shouldn't be heated empty for long (see [Making non-stick pans last](../non-stick-pan-care/)). Heat the pan over medium-high, add a little oil and put the meat in when the oil shimmers. It should sizzle straight away; if it doesn't, take it out and wait. Salt just before cooking, not long before, because salt draws out moisture.

**Pieces and mince:** lay pieces down away from you, leave them for a few minutes, and turn them only once they come away easily. For mince, spread it out in one layer, let it sit until the underside is brown, then break it up with a wooden spoon and brown again, rather than stirring constantly. A large pan takes about 500 g of mince at a time. Pour off excess fat before you add other ingredients.

**Deglazing:** lift the meat out, pour off most of the fat, then add a splash of stock, wine or water to the hot pan and scrape the bottom as it bubbles. The fond dissolves into the liquid, which goes back into the dish with the meat (see [Slow-simmering a sauce](../simmering-sauces/)). Deep brown fond is good, but if it has turned black and smells bitter, wipe the pan out instead.

**Staying safe:** dry meat spatters less. Keep handles turned in, use long tongs, and keep a lid nearby: if oil catches fire, turn off the heat and cover the pan, and never throw water on it. Then finish the cooking. Whole steaks and chops can be pink inside, but mince, sausages and chicken need 75 °C in the centre (see [Cook by temperature, not time](../cook-by-temperature/)).
