## Summary

This pattern is for negotiating teams and the ministers they advise. It describes how to work out your fallback and your bottom line, understand the other side's, and use trade-offs across chapters so that you neither cave in nor walk out without a good reason.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Work out your BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, and keep improving it. | Your alternatives, not your arguments, decide how much pressure you can withstand and when a deal is worth taking. |
| Set a reservation point for each key issue: the least favourable outcome you'd still accept. | A bottom line agreed in advance stops you sliding past it one small concession at a time. |
| Analyse the counterpart's interests, alternatives and domestic constraints. | Knowing what their parliament, farmers or industries will accept shows you what's possible and where to push. |
| Look past positions to interests, and trade across chapters. | A demand that's impossible in one chapter can often be met by giving something the other side values in another. |
| Don't bid against yourself: wait for a response before improving your offer. | Moving twice in a row teaches the counterpart that waiting pays. |

## Context

This pattern suits the whole negotiation, but especially the end game, when deadlines, summits and political announcements create pressure to close.

It does not suit treating the walkaway as a threat to brandish at every meeting: a BATNA is for your own judgement. The hard limits themselves belong in [the negotiating mandate](../trade-negotiating-mandate/), and this pattern is about applying them at the table.

There's a trade-off between ambition and closure. Holding out for more may get a better deal or none at all, and an agreement that's too ambitious may not survive ratification at home. Walking away is a legitimate outcome, and it isn't always final. Talks between Australia and the EU broke down in 2023 over agricultural market access, then resumed and concluded in March 2026.

## Usage

**Working out your alternatives:** for a trade agreement, the alternative to a deal is rarely nothing. It might be your existing agreements, the WTO terms the parties already apply to each other, a regional agreement such as RCEP or the CPTPP, or other markets for your exporters. Write down what each would actually deliver, sector by sector, using what you learned from [consulting stakeholders](../trade-stakeholder-consultation/).

**Reading the other side:** ask what the counterpart's government needs to take home. That might be protection for a sensitive industry or a timetable set by its own elections. Its constraints limit what it can offer, and they also tell you which concessions it will value most.

**Separating interests from positions:** a position is what a side demands, such as "no change to this tariff". An interest is the reason behind it, such as protecting regional jobs. Longer phase-in periods, quotas or safeguards can often meet the interest while moving the position.

**Linking concessions:** keep a record of what you offered in exchange for what, so that a concession on one chapter isn't banked without its counterpart. The [single negotiating text](../single-negotiating-text/) and the rule that nothing is agreed until everything is agreed help you do this.
