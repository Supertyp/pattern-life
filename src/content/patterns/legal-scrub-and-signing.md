## Summary

This pattern is for negotiating teams and treaty lawyers in the months between the announcement that talks have concluded and the signing ceremony. It describes how to turn the negotiated text into a final treaty text without reopening the deal.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Run a joint legal scrub of the whole text, with lawyers from both sides checking defined terms, cross-references and treaty language. | Chapters drafted by different working groups drift apart, and an error that survives signature binds you for decades. |
| Agree at the outset that the scrub fixes drafting, not substance, and send anything substantive back to the chief negotiators. | Without that rule, the scrub becomes a quiet extra negotiating round and the deal can unravel. |
| Plan translation and verification into every authentic language early. | Each authentic language version carries equal legal weight, so a mistranslated obligation is a real obligation. |
| Seek approval to sign in good time. In Australia that means the relevant ministers and then the Federal Executive Council. | DFAT advises allowing about eight weeks after conclusion for this step, and signing can't happen without it. |
| Publish the text, and explain plainly what signature does and doesn't commit you to. | Stakeholders and Parliament can only scrutinise what they can read, and people often think signature means the deal is already in force. |

## Context

This pattern suits comprehensive bilateral or regional agreements negotiated chapter by chapter. It follows on from [Working from a single negotiating text](../single-negotiating-text/).

It does not suit short, routine treaty actions such as technical amendments, which follow a much lighter process.

There's a trade-off between speed and polish. Political leaders often want to sign soon after talks conclude, while a thorough scrub and translation take months. Signing known errors is worse than waiting, but a long gap invites attempts to reopen settled issues.

## Usage

**Running the scrub:** after conclusion, the text is "frozen". Lawyers from both sides work through it together, aligning defined terms (for example "originating good"), checking every cross-reference and annex, and confirming that the schedules match the chapters. (Schedules list every tariff line, meaning each product code in the customs tariff, with its phase-out of duties.) Log every change and have the chief negotiators confirm the log.

**Translation and certification:** if the treaty is authentic in more than one language, the versions have to say the same thing. In Australian practice the partner supplies the translation and your side verifies it. An EU agreement must be translated into all 24 official EU languages before signature. Only when every version has been checked do the parties settle the final texts that will be signed.

**What signature means:** under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, signature subject to ratification doesn't bind a state to the agreement. It does oblige the state not to act against the treaty's object and purpose until it makes clear it won't become a party (Article 18). In Australia, signature opens parliamentary scrutiny. The Australia–EU agreement, concluded on 24 March 2026, is an example: both sides published a provisional text marked as subject to legal and linguistic revision and final only on signature. The next step is [Ratification and entry into force](../ratification-and-entry-into-force/).
