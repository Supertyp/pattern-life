## Summary

This pattern is for anyone stitching hand embroidery or cross-stitch. It describes three reliable ways to start a thread without leaving a knot behind, and one way to end it, so the front lies flat and the back stays tidy.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with a waste knot or away knot on the front of the fabric, and cut it off once the thread is secure. | The knot only holds the thread temporarily, so no lump is left on the back. |
| When stitching with an even number of strands, start with a loop. | It anchors the thread in the first stitch with no knot and no tail. |
| End by running the thread under three or four stitches on the back, then trim it close. | The friction of the surrounding stitches holds it, and there's no knot to pull through or show. |
| Don't carry the thread more than about 2 cm across bare fabric on the back; end it and start again. | Long carries show as shadows through light fabric and can snag. |
| Use a small knot only on items that will be worn and washed often. | Frequent laundering can loosen woven-in tails, and a knot adds security where looks matter less. |

## Context

This pattern suits framed or displayed pieces, samplers, cross-stitch and most surface embroidery.

It does not suit clothing, tea towels and other things that will be washed hard and often, where many stitchers prefer a small knot, ideally hidden under stitching.

There's a trade-off between speed and a neat back. A plain knot is quickest, but it can show as a bump, slip through loosely woven fabric or tangle later stitches. The methods here take a few seconds longer. Stitchers do disagree: some happily use knots on everything, and few people ever see the back of a framed piece. This pattern avoids knots because the front lies flatter, and suggests a knot only for hard-wearing items.

## Usage

**Waste knot:** tie a knot in the end of the thread. Push the needle down from the front about 2.5 cm from where you'll start, in the path of the stitches you're about to make, so the knot sits on top. Stitch towards and over the thread on the back, which locks it in. Once it's covered, snip the knot off close to the fabric. This suits fills such as [satin stitch](../satin-stitch/).

**Away knot:** for lines and curves, put the knot on the front about 8 to 10 cm away from the design, outside the stitching area. Stitch as normal. When you've finished that thread, cut off the knot, turn the work over, thread the tail onto the needle and weave it under the back of a few stitches.

**Loop start:** for two strands, separate a single strand about twice your working length (see [Managing floss](../managing-thread/)), fold it in half and thread both cut ends through the needle, leaving the loop at the other end. Bring the needle up from the back where the first stitch starts, leaving the loop hanging behind. Make your first stitch down to the back, pass the needle through the waiting loop and pull gently. For four strands, fold two strands in the same way. It works best where the first stitch is a straight stitch, as in [back stitch](../back-stitch/) or [cross-stitch](../cross-stitch-basics/).

**Ending:** with 10 to 15 cm of thread left, take the needle to the back. Slide it under the backs of three or four nearby stitches, change direction and go back under one or two more, then cut it close. On fills, weave under stitches of the same colour so dark thread doesn't shadow through. Plan your stitching order so you don't need long jumps (see [Planning a design](../planning-stitch-order/)).
