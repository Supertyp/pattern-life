## Summary

This pattern is for officials preparing to negotiate a trade agreement. It describes how to scope the agreement and get a formally approved mandate before the first round, so that the team knows what it's trying to achieve, what it must protect and what it can't agree to.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Set objectives chapter by chapter (goods, services, investment, rules of origin, digital trade, labour and environment), listing both offensive and defensive interests. | Each chapter has its own working group and trade-offs, and a team that only knows what it wants to win can be caught out on what it needs to protect. |
| Base the objectives on evidence: trade data, the counterpart's actual barriers and realistic economic modelling. | Evidence shows where the commercial value really lies and keeps expectations honest when results are reported later. |
| Agree red lines in advance and record them in the mandate. | Limits set calmly at home hold up better than limits invented under pressure at the table. |
| Say explicitly what is out of scope. | Topics left vague tend to creep into the talks, and the counterpart will read silence as openness. |
| Get the mandate formally approved by government before talks start. | Negotiators can only make credible offers if the counterpart believes their government will stand behind them. |

## Context

This pattern suits new bilateral or regional agreements, and major upgrades of existing ones, where the scope is open and several portfolios are involved.

It does not suit narrow technical arrangements, such as a mutual recognition agreement for one profession, where a lighter ministerial approval is enough. It also isn't a substitute for consulting the people affected, which runs alongside it (see [Consulting stakeholders](../trade-stakeholder-consultation/)).

There's a trade-off between detail and flexibility. A tightly written mandate protects sensitive sectors and makes the team accountable, but it can leave negotiators with nothing to trade when the counterpart moves. Most governments keep the red lines firm and the targets for each chapter as ranges, and they go back for a revised mandate if the deal changes shape.

## Usage

**Building the mandate:** ask each lead agency to write up its chapter: what it wants, what it fears and the evidence for both. List the "tariff lines" (single product categories in a customs tariff) that matter most to exporters and to sensitive industries. "Rules of origin" decide how much of a product must be made in a member country to qualify for lower tariffs, and they often matter as much as the tariff cuts themselves.

**Keeping the evidence honest:** Australia's Productivity Commission found in 2010 that the benefits of bilateral agreements had been oversold and recommended realistic, independently overseen modelling. Use modelling to compare options and rank priorities, not to promise a headline figure.

**Getting approval:** in Australia the lead minister seeks a mandate from the Minister for Foreign Affairs, or from Cabinet, before formal negotiations start. In the EU the European Commission asks the Council for authorisation, and the Council's negotiating directives act as the mandate. The EU has published several of its mandates, including the one for the Australia–EU talks.

**Using it:** when you know your limits you can work out your fallback position (see [Knowing your walkaway](../know-your-walkaway/)), and the chapter objectives become the structure of the [single negotiating text](../single-negotiating-text/).
