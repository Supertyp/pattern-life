## Summary

This pattern is for negotiating teams and the officials who support them. It describes how to consult before and during negotiations so that you hear from everyone affected, use what they tell you and stay trusted, without publishing your negotiating position.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Consult widely: industry, unions, states and territories, First Nations groups, civil society and the public. | Each group knows barriers and risks that the others don't, and those left out are the ones who object loudest later. |
| Open public submissions before the first round and keep them open throughout. | Exporters and affected communities can tell you about problems on the ground that no trade data will show. |
| Brief stakeholders regularly after rounds, and share sensitive detail only with cleared advisers. | Regular briefings keep trust without exposing your position, and advisers who have signed confidentiality undertakings can comment on real offers. |
| Report back on what you heard and how it shaped your approach. | People keep engaging when they can see their input was read, even when it didn't win. |

## Context

This pattern suits comprehensive agreements that reach beyond tariffs into services, investment, intellectual property, labour or the environment, where many groups have a stake.

It does not suit purely technical updates, such as transposing tariff schedules into a new customs nomenclature, where a targeted notice to the affected industry is enough. It also can't replace the formal parliamentary scrutiny that comes later (see [Ratification and entry into force](../ratification-and-entry-into-force/)).

There's a trade-off between transparency and confidentiality. Publishing your offers would weaken your hand, but a process that feels like a black box loses public trust and makes ratification harder. Most governments publish their objectives, round summaries and, after conclusion, the text, while sharing live offers only with cleared advisers.

## Usage

**Before the talks:** call for submissions when talks are announced, and feed what you learn into [the negotiating mandate](../trade-negotiating-mandate/). In Australia, DFAT registers people who make submissions as stakeholders for follow-up and publishes the submissions online. For the Australia–EU agreement it received submissions from industry bodies, unions, researchers and advocacy groups over the eight years of negotiation.

**During the talks:** hold briefings after each round for all interested groups, and run sector sessions on sensitive chapters. Work closely with states and territories on anything that touches their powers, such as procurement or services regulation. Consult First Nations groups through their own organisations and meetings, not only through general submissions. Systems differ: the US has a standing network of cleared trade advisory committees set up by Congress, while the European Commission holds civil society dialogue meetings and publishes round reports.

**Reporting back:** after each round, say which chapters moved and which concerns you're pursuing, without revealing what you'll concede. What you hear about sensitive sectors is direct input to [knowing your walkaway](../know-your-walkaway/).
