## Summary

This pattern is for anyone about to stitch a hand embroidery design. It describes how to match each part of the design to a stitch, and in which order to work the parts so that later stitches don't spoil earlier ones.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Before you thread a needle, mark up a copy of the design with the stitch and colour for each element. | Decisions made at the table are calmer and more consistent than decisions made mid-stitch, and you can see the whole piece at once. |
| Choose stitches by the kind of line or shape: straight lines, curves, solid fills, shaded fills or dots. | Each stitch has a job it does best, so a well-matched stitch looks neat with less effort. |
| Work from the background to the foreground. | Elements in front then overlap the ones behind, the way they would in real life, and nothing in front gets crushed or snagged. |
| Try any unfamiliar stitch on scrap fabric first. | A small test sampler shows how many strands and what stitch length look right before you commit. |
| Plan your route so the thread never jumps a long way across bare fabric on the back. | Long carried threads show through light fabric and can snag and pull the stitches on the front. |

## Context

This pattern suits freestyle (surface) embroidery, where you choose the stitches yourself, from a small hoop to a larger wall piece.

It does not suit counted cross-stitch, where the chart sets the stitches and the order is mostly about colour and area (see [Counted cross-stitch basics](../cross-stitch-basics/)), or kits whose instructions already give a stitch order.

There's a trade-off with outlines next to fills. Working the fill first and the outline last lets the outline hide a slightly ragged fill edge and stops fill stitches snagging the outline. Working the outline first gives you a clear boundary to stitch up to, and a split-stitch edge under satin stitch helps keep its edge crisp. Practitioners differ, and many say either works when the outline isn't touching a fill. This pattern suggests fills first and visible outlines last, but choose outline first when you need the guide line, or when the edge will be covered anyway.

## Usage

**Choosing stitches:** look at each element and ask what it is. For straight or gently curved lines and lettering, use [Back stitch](../back-stitch/). For flowing curves such as stems, use [Stem stitch](../stem-stitch/). Fill small, solid shapes with [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/), and larger or shaded shapes with [Long-and-short stitch](../long-and-short-stitch/). Petals and leaves suit [Lazy daisy](../lazy-daisy-stitch/), and dots, eyes and flower centres suit [French knots](../french-knot/). Write the stitch name and the thread colour number beside each part of a printed copy. Use more strands for bold lines in front and fewer for fine detail or things further away.

**Order of work:** first the things furthest back (sky, distant hills, a large background shape), then the middle, then the foreground. Within one element, do fills before visible outlines. Leave raised or textured stitches, such as French knots, to the very end, because the hoop and your hand would flatten them while you work the rest.

**Test sampler:** keep a strip of the same fabric in a spare hoop. Stitch a few centimetres of each stitch with the strands you plan to use and check how it looks from arm's length. This takes ten minutes and saves unpicking.

**Routing the thread:** work all the areas of one colour that sit close together, then end the thread. If the next area is more than 2 to 3 cm away, or the gap is bare fabric, finish off and start again rather than carry the thread across (see [Starting and ending threads neatly](../starting-and-ending-threads/)). Short moves are fine if you weave them under existing stitches on the back. As with [preparing before you cook](../mise-en-place/), sorting your threads and needles before you start keeps the plan easy to follow.
