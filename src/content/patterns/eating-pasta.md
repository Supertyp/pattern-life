## Summary

This pattern is for anyone eating a plate or bowl of pasta in company. It describes how to twirl long pasta into a neat mouthful, how to handle short shapes, and the small habits that keep the sauce off you and the table.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Twirl long pasta with your fork against the side or base of the plate or bowl, taking only a few strands at a time. | A few strands make a compact mouthful. A forkful that's too big unravels, drips and has to be slurped. |
| Don't cut long pasta with a knife or the side of your fork. | Twirling works if the portion is small enough, and chopped strands are harder to pick up and, in Italy, a well-known faux pas. |
| Spear or scoop short shapes with the fork. | Tubes, shells and twists hold their sauce and need no twirling. |
| Use a spoon only where it's the custom or you need the help. | It's common in some countries and fine at home, but unusual at an Italian table, where the fork alone is the norm. |
| Keep your napkin on your lap, lean slightly over the plate and eat while it's hot. | Drips land on the plate rather than on you, and pasta thickens and clumps as it cools. |

## Context

This pattern suits long and short pasta in sauce, eaten at home, at friends' houses and in restaurants.

It does not suit pasta in broth or soups such as minestrone, which you eat with a spoon, or baked dishes such as lasagne, which you cut with the side of your fork. Customs differ between countries and families: in parts of the United States and among some Italian-American families, twirling against a spoon is normal, and nobody should feel judged for it at their own table.

There's a trade-off between etiquette and comfort. The fork-only method takes a little practice, while a spoon makes twirling easier but looks out of place in Italy. Children and anyone still learning can use a spoon, and moving to fork-only is easy once the twirl feels natural.

## Usage

**The twirl:** separate a few strands from the edge of the pile, not the middle, with the tips of your fork. Rest the tines against the side of the bowl or a clear patch of the plate, and turn the fork so the strands wind around it. Stop when the bundle is a mouthful. If loose ends hang down, turn once more or lift and let them drop, rather than sucking them in.

**Short shapes:** spear one or two pieces with the tines, or turn the fork over and scoop, the way you would rice. Use a piece of bread or your fork, not your fingers, to collect sauce from the plate (in Italy, mopping it up with bread is called *fare la scarpetta* and is normal at home).

**Setting up for success:** a warmed, shallow bowl helps a lot, because the curved side gives you something to twirl against (see [Setting the table](../setting-the-table/)). Pasta that has been tossed in its sauce clings together and twirls more easily than strands with sauce spooned on top (see [Bringing pasta and sauce together](../saucing-pasta/)), and so does pasta cooked al dente (see [Boiling pasta](../boiling-pasta/)).

**Cheese:** add grated cheese from the bowl on the table with its own spoon, sprinkling a little at a time. Traditionally, Italians don't add cheese to seafood pasta.
