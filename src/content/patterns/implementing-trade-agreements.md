## Summary

This pattern is for officials, customs agencies and industry bodies responsible for an agreement once it has entered into force. It describes how to make the agreement work, measure its use and fix problems through its own institutions.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Load the tariff schedules and rules of origin into customs systems before entry into force, and publish guidance for importers. | Preferences only reach traders if the customs system applies the right rate on day one. |
| Give businesses plain guidance and tools: a tariff lookup, an origin self-check and a sample declaration of origin. | Preferences aren't automatic, and smaller firms in particular often don't know how to claim them. |
| Track preference utilisation rates by partner and sector, and follow up where they're low. | They show whether the agreement is actually used, not just signed. |
| Use the joint committee and the built-in review clauses to fix problems and update the agreement. | Most problems are technical and can be solved by officials who meet regularly. |
| Raise problems through consultation first, and treat formal dispute settlement as a last resort. | Formal disputes are slow and adversarial, and consultation usually resolves issues faster. |

## Context

This pattern suits any trade agreement with tariff preferences, rules of origin or ongoing committees. It follows [Ratification and entry into force](../ratification-and-entry-into-force/).

It does not suit purely political declarations or framework agreements without market access commitments, where there's little to put into customs systems.

There's a trade-off between promoting the agreement and assessing it honestly. Governments naturally highlight the gains, but people trust the data more when weak spots are published too. These findings should feed back into the next [negotiating mandate](../trade-negotiating-mandate/).

## Usage

**Customs and guidance:** rules of origin are the tests a product must pass to count as made in a member country, for example a change of tariff classification during manufacture. Customs needs every tariff line (the individual product code in the customs tariff) and its phase-out loaded and tested before day one. In Australia, the Australian Border Force publishes a guide to claiming preferences for each agreement, and DFAT's FTA Portal lets exporters look up rates, check the origin rules and see what proof of origin they need.

**Measuring use:** the preference utilisation rate is the share of eligible trade that actually claims the lower tariff. Exchange this data with your partner yearly, by sector. Low rates usually point to small tariff margins, complex origin rules or businesses that don't know about the agreement. For the Australia–UK FTA, the two governments reported that between June 2023 and December 2024 about 65% of eligible UK goods entering Australia and 77% of eligible Australian goods entering the UK used the preferences.

**Committees and disputes:** most agreements set up a joint committee of ministers or senior officials, with working groups beneath it. Use them to raise problems, agree technical fixes and run any scheduled review. Bring industry and union advisers in through the [consultation arrangements](../trade-stakeholder-consultation/) you set up during negotiations. If a partner isn't meeting an obligation, start with formal consultations. State-to-state dispute settlement under bilateral agreements is rarely used, and it works best as a backstop.
