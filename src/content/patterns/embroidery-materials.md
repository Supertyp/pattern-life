## Summary

This pattern is for anyone gathering supplies for hand embroidery. It describes how to pick a fabric, a thread and a needle that suit each other, so the materials help rather than hinder.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| For surface embroidery, choose a firm, closely woven, non-stretchy fabric such as medium-weight cotton or linen. | A stable weave holds stitches in place and doesn't pucker the way knits and stretchy fabrics do. |
| For counted cross-stitch, use Aida, and start with 14-count. | Aida's square blocks and visible holes make counting easy, and 14-count is large enough to see comfortably. |
| Use six-stranded cotton (floss) and split it, usually working with two or three strands. | Splitting lets you choose the line weight, and two or three strands suit most designs on medium fabric. |
| Use a sharp embroidery (crewel) needle for surface work, a blunt tapestry needle for Aida and other counted fabrics, and a chenille needle for thick threads on heavy fabric. | The point decides whether the needle pierces the fibres or slips between the holes, and the eye has to suit the thread. |
| Pick a needle just big enough for the thread: about size 7 to 9 for two or three strands of floss. | A needle that's too small shreds the thread, and one that's too large leaves holes around every stitch. |

## Context

This pattern suits beginners and anyone choosing materials for a new piece of surface embroidery or counted cross-stitch.

It does not suit specialist work with its own conventions, such as goldwork, silk ribbon embroidery, crewelwork in wool or needlepoint on canvas. Follow a specialist guide or the kit instructions for those.

There's a trade-off between fabric that's easy and fabric that's beautiful. Quilting cotton is cheap, forgiving and easy to trace onto, while linen has a lovely texture but frays and shows uneven tension more. This pattern suggests starting with a medium-weight cotton or cotton-linen blend, and moving to linen once your tension is steady. Strand counts are a matter of taste too: patterns and teachers differ, so treat two or three strands as a starting point, not a rule.

## Usage

**Fabric:** wash and iron the fabric before you start, because some fabrics shrink or run, and Aida often carries a stiffening dressing. Cut it at least 5 cm bigger than your hoop on every side so it can be gripped and later finished (see [Hooping fabric drum-tight](../hooping-fabric/)). If you must stitch on a T-shirt or other stretchy fabric, back it with an iron-on or water-soluble stabiliser first.

**Thread:** stranded cotton, also called floss, comes in skeins of six fine strands twisted loosely together. You almost never stitch with all six. One strand gives fine detail, two or three suit most outlines and fills, and more strands give bold, raised lines. Cut and separate it as described in [Managing floss](../managing-thread/). Match the weight of the thread to the fabric: fine threads on light fabric, heavier threads on heavier fabric.

**Needle:** needle sizes run backwards, so the higher the number, the finer the needle. Embroidery or crewel needles (sizes 1 to 12) have a sharp point and a long eye for floss. Tapestry needles (roughly 13 to 28) have a large eye and blunt tip that slides between Aida's holes without splitting threads; size 24 or 26 suits 14-count Aida (see [Counted cross-stitch basics](../cross-stitch-basics/)). Chenille needles share the tapestry sizes but have a sharp point for wool or perle thread on heavy fabric. A mixed packet of embroidery needles, sizes 3 to 9, covers most beginners' needs.

**Testing:** before you commit, stitch a few lines on a scrap of the same fabric. If the needle drags, go up a size; if you can see holes around the stitches, go down one. Lay everything out first, as in [Mise en place](../mise-en-place/).
