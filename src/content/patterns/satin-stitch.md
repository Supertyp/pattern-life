## Summary

This pattern is for anyone filling small shapes with satin stitch: straight stitches laid side by side so they cover the fabric completely. It describes how to get crisp edges, an even slant and stitches that stay put.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Outline the shape in split stitch first and take each satin stitch just over the outline. | The outline gives a firm edge to stitch against, so the edge stays smooth instead of nibbled. |
| Start in the middle of the shape at the angle you want, work to one end, then return to the middle and work to the other end. | Setting the angle in the middle keeps the slant even across the whole shape. |
| Lay each stitch right beside the last, flat and untwisted, without overlapping. | Parallel, flat stitches reflect light evenly, which is what makes satin stitch shine. |
| Keep stitches short, ideally under 1 cm, and pad or split anything wider. | Long stitches loosen, shift and snag once the work is out of the hoop. |
| Use fewer strands (one or two) for a smoother finish. | Thick bundles of strands twist and sit unevenly; fine thread lies flat. |

## Context

This pattern suits leaves, petals, dots, small letters and other compact shapes, especially on items that are framed rather than washed and worn.

It does not suit large areas, where stitches would be too long; use [Long-and-short stitch](../long-and-short-stitch/) or rows of [Stem stitch](../stem-stitch/) instead. It's also a poor choice for items that get heavy wear, such as bag straps.

There's a trade-off between speed and smoothness. Teachers differ on the maximum length: some allow up to about 2.5 cm when the shape is padded, while others keep unpadded stitches under 1 cm. This pattern stays conservative (under 1 cm, padding from about 5 mm), because shorter stitches survive handling and washing. More strands cover faster but look less smooth; choose two or three strands for quick, casual pieces and one strand when the finish matters.

## Usage

**Outline:** split stitch is a short line stitch where each new stitch comes up through the middle of the previous one, splitting its strands. Work it along the drawn line with stitches about 2 to 3 mm long, in the same colour as the fill.

**Padding:** for shapes wider than about 5 mm, fill the inside of the outline with a layer of padding first: a few rows of split stitch, or straight stitches running at right angles to the final satin stitch direction. Padding raises the surface and gives friction, which holds the satin stitches in place and makes them look plump.

**Filling:** decide the stitch direction; across the narrow width of the shape usually works best, and at an angle looks lively on leaves. Bring the needle up just outside the outline at the middle of the shape, and go down just outside the outline on the opposite side, angling the needle slightly under the outline. Then come up again on the **same** side you started, right next to the first stitch. This covers the back as well as the front, which gives a fuller surface. Work stitch by stitch to one end, then go back to the middle and fill the other half. Pull each stitch snug, but not so tight that the fabric puckers. If the strands twist, let the needle dangle to untwist them (see [Managing floss](../managing-thread/)) or stroke them flat with a spare needle as you pull.

**Finishing the edges:** if an edge still looks uneven, outline it afterwards in [Back stitch](../back-stitch/) or [Stem stitch](../stem-stitch/), or add [French knots](../french-knot/) for decoration. Plan outlines and fills together (see [Planning a design](../planning-stitch-order/)).
