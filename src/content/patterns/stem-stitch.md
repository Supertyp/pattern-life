## Summary

This pattern is for anyone embroidering curved lines by hand. It describes how to work stem stitch, an overlapping line stitch that looks like a twisted cord, so that curves stay smooth and the line keeps an even thickness.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep the working thread on the same side of the needle for the whole line. | Swapping sides breaks the twist, and the line suddenly looks like a different stitch. |
| Bring each new stitch up in the hole at the end of the previous stitch, so each stitch overlaps half of the one before. | The overlap creates the rope effect and hides the gaps between stitches. |
| Keep stitches the same length on straight sections, about 3 to 4 mm. | Even lengths give an even line; mixed lengths make it look lumpy. |
| Shorten the stitches to about 2 mm on tight curves. | Short stitches bend round the curve; long ones cut across it and make corners. |
| Treat a sharp point as two lines: end one line at the point and start a new one. | Stem stitch can't turn a sharp corner without bunching. |

## Context

This pattern suits outlines, flower stems, vines, curly lettering and filling a shape with close rows of stem stitch. It works best on fabric hooped drum-tight (see [Hooping fabric drum-tight](../hooping-fabric/)) with two or three strands of stranded cotton, the six-strand embroidery floss sold in skeins.

It does not suit very short details (one or two stitches can't form the twist), lines with many sharp angles, where [Back stitch](../back-stitch/) is crisper, or solid shapes, which suit [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/).

There's a trade-off in which side the thread sits. Keeping the thread below the needle (towards you) when working left to right gives the classic rope, and is what most teachers recommend with stranded cotton; keeping it above gives a slightly flatter line that some books call outline stitch. This pattern uses thread below, but either is fine as long as you don't switch within a line.

## Usage

**Working the line:** draw the line on the fabric (see [Transferring a design to fabric](../transferring-designs/)). Work from left to right if you're right-handed. Bring the needle up at the start of the line. Go down about 4 mm along the line, and before pulling tight, check the thread lies below the needle. Come up halfway back, about 2 mm from the start, right on the line, and pull the thread through. For the next stitch, go down 4 mm ahead of where you just came up (2 mm beyond the end of the first stitch), then come up in the hole at the end of the first stitch. Repeat: down one stitch ahead, up in the previous end hole, thread always below.

**Curves:** turn the hoop so you're always stitching in roughly the same direction, and let the stitches follow the drawn curve. On tighter bends, drop to 2 mm stitches so they hug each other. Keep the thread on the same side as before; only the angle of the stitches changes.

**Ending and thickness:** finish by taking the last stitch down exactly on the end of the line, then secure the thread on the back (see [Starting and ending threads neatly](../starting-and-ending-threads/)). For a thicker line use more strands, not longer stitches. Three strands of stranded cotton make a bold line; one strand makes a fine, delicate one. Untwisted, well-managed floss gives a neater rope (see [Managing floss](../managing-thread/)).

**Filling with stem stitch:** for a filled shape, work rows of stem stitch side by side, following the outline inwards, with the thread on the same side in every row. This gives a textured fill that bends round curves better than [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/).
