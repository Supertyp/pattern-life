## Summary

This pattern is for anyone starting thread painting, also called needle painting or silk shading. It describes long-and-short stitch, the main stitch behind it: rows of straight stitches of staggered lengths that blend one shade into the next while following the direction a petal or leaf grows.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Outline the shape in split stitch and draw stitch direction lines before you start. | The outline gives a crisp edge, and the lines keep every stitch pointing the way the shape grows. |
| Work the first row with alternating long and short stitches over the outline, starting in the centre of the edge. | Staggered lengths create the soft, uneven boundary that the next colour blends into. |
| In later rows, split into the previous row's stitches at staggered points, not into the bare fabric between rows. | Splitting knits the rows together, so no gaps or hard lines appear between shades. |
| Use one strand and step through close shades, one shade per row. | Fine thread and small jumps in colour give a smooth blend; big jumps look like stripes. |
| Fan the stitch angle gradually round curves, adding short extra stitches where needed. | Sudden changes of direction show as breaks in the surface. |

## Context

This pattern suits realistic flowers, leaves, birds, animals and any larger shape where you want shading or a smooth fill too wide for [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/). It's slow work, so plan breaks (see [Stitching comfortably](../stitching-comfortably/)).

It does not suit quick, bold, graphic designs, where satin stitch or rows of [Stem stitch](../stem-stitch/) are faster, or loosely woven fabric.

There's a trade-off in how you join rows. Some teachers, such as Mary Corbet, bring the needle up through the previous row's stitches and go down further into the shape; the Royal School of Needlework method instead takes the needle down into the previous row. Both blend well. This pattern describes coming up through the previous row, which many beginners find easier to place, but follow one method within a piece.

## Usage

**Preparing:** transfer the outline and pencil in a few direction lines that fan towards the centre of the flower or along the leaf vein (see [Transferring a design to fabric](../transferring-designs/)). Choose four or five shades of one colour, close in tone. Use one strand of stranded cotton in a size 9 or 10 embroidery needle (see [Choosing fabric, thread and needle](../embroidery-materials/)). Work a split stitch outline along the outer edge in the first shade, with stitches about 2 to 3 mm long.

**First row:** start at the centre of the outer edge. Bring the needle up inside the shape about 1 cm from the edge and go down just over the split stitch outline, following the direction line. Next to it, make a shorter stitch, about 6 mm. Keep alternating long and short stitches, lying close together like satin stitch, out to one end of the edge; then return to the centre and work the other way. Vary the lengths slightly so the inner edge is ragged.

**Next rows:** change to the next shade. Bring the needle up through the stitches of the previous row, splitting them about a third of the way back from their inner ends, and go down into the bare fabric further into the shape. In this and later rows the stitches are similar lengths (about 8 to 12 mm); the staggering comes from where they start. Again work from the centre outwards. To soften a boundary, slip a few stitches of the new shade into the end of the previous row. Repeat, one shade per row, until the shape is full, adjusting the angle gradually and adding short "wedge" stitches where stitches spread apart on a curve.

**Checking:** step back after every row. Long-and-short is forgiving: a hard line or gap can usually be fixed by adding stitches rather than unpicking.
