## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks with chopped vegetables. It describes a simple, repeatable way to cut them into even cubes of the size a dish needs, quickly and without putting your fingers at risk.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pick a size for the dish: fine (about 3 mm) to melt into a sauce, small (about 5 mm) for soups, fried rice and salsa, medium (about 1 cm) for stews and roasting trays. | The size decides how fast a piece cooks and whether it disappears into the dish or stays a distinct bite. |
| Keep every piece about the same size. | Even pieces cook at the same rate, so nothing burns while you wait for the rest to soften. |
| Square off the vegetable, then cut planks, then sticks, then cubes. | A flat side stops it rolling, and cutting to an even width at each step gives even cubes almost automatically. |
| For onions, halve through the root and leave the root on while you cut. | The root holds the layers together, so the half stays in one piece until your final cross-cuts. |
| Use a sharp chef's knife, a board that can't slip and a claw grip. | Dicing means many cuts close to your fingers, and a sharp blade goes where you aim it. |

## Context

This pattern suits everyday cooking: the onion, carrot and celery base of soups and stews, potatoes for a hash, capsicum and tomato for a salsa, or vegetables for a stir-fry or curry.

It does not suit soft or leafy produce such as herbs, spinach or ripe tomatoes, which are better sliced or roughly chopped, or garlic and ginger, which are usually minced or grated.

There's a trade-off between neatness and speed. Squaring off a carrot or potato wastes a little and takes longer than hacking at it. For a long-simmered soup or a blended sauce, a rough chop is fine. Save careful dicing for dishes where the pieces stay visible, cook quickly or need to cook evenly, and keep the trimmings for stock.

## Usage

**Getting set up:** before you start, check your knife is sharp (see [Keeping knives sharp](../knife-sharpening/)) and put a damp cloth under the board (see [Stable board, claw grip](../safe-cutting/)). Have a bowl ready for each vegetable, so the diced pieces are waiting in order when the pan is hot (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)).

**Onions:** cut off the stem end, then halve the onion from top to root and peel off the skin. Lay one half flat side down with the root pointing away from you. Make vertical cuts from just in front of the root towards you, spaced at your chosen dice size. If you want a fine dice, add one or two horizontal cuts towards the root, keeping your flat hand on top and well above the blade. Then slice across the cuts to release the cubes, and throw away the root.

**Carrots, celery and potatoes:** for a carrot or potato, cut a thin slice off one side so it lies flat, then square off the other sides. Cut it lengthways into planks as thick as your dice, stack or lay the planks flat, cut them into sticks of the same width, then line the sticks up and cut across them into cubes. Celery is already a flat plank: cut long thin strips, then cut across.

**Getting faster:** keep the tip of the knife on the board and rock or push the blade forward through the food, rather than chopping straight down. Speed comes from rhythm and practice, not force. Go slowly until your cuts are even, and the speed will come.
