## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who makes a sauce, stew, curry or stock that cooks for a long time on the stove. It describes how to keep it at a gentle simmer, thicken it, season it and cool it safely afterwards.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a heavy-based pot and keep it at a gentle simmer, with just an occasional bubble breaking the surface, not a boil. | A thick base spreads the heat so the bottom doesn't scorch, and a gentle heat lets meat turn tender and flavours blend, where boiling toughens meat, clouds stock and makes fat separate. |
| Leave the lid ajar or off to thicken, and stir and scrape the bottom every 15 to 20 minutes. | Steam needs to escape for the liquid to reduce, and scraping stops a layer catching and burning on the base. |
| Add liquid in stages rather than all at once, topping up only if it gets too thick before it's done. | You keep control of the thickness, and a sauce that's drowned at the start takes far longer to reduce. |
| Skim off fat and foam, and taste and adjust the salt and acidity near the end. | The liquid reduces and concentrates, so salt added early can end up too strong, and excess fat makes a dish heavy and greasy. |
| Make it a day ahead if you can, and cool it quickly into the fridge. | Flavours mellow and blend overnight, the fat sets on top so it's easy to lift off, and fast cooling keeps it safe. |

## Context

This pattern suits tomato sauces, meat sauces such as a [bolognese](../bolognese/), vegetable sauces, stocks, stews, braises, soups and curries.

It does not suit quick pan sauces, delicate fish or vegetables that fall apart with long cooking, or sauces thickened with egg or a lot of cream, which can split if they simmer for long.

There's a trade-off between time and attention. A long, slow simmer gives the deepest flavour and the most tender meat, but it ties you to the kitchen. A low oven at about 150 °C, a slow cooker or a pressure cooker needs less stirring, though a sauce reduces less with the lid on and may need a final simmer uncovered to thicken.

## Usage

**Finding the simmer:** bring the pot up to a bubble, then turn the heat right down until only a few small bubbles break the surface. On a strong burner, use the smallest ring, move the pot off-centre or use a heat diffuser. Check it after 10 minutes, as a pot can creep back to the boil once it's fully hot. A heavy enamelled cast-iron pot is ideal (see [Caring for cast iron](../cast-iron-care/)).

**Thickening and topping up:** for a tomato sauce or stew, simmer uncovered or with the lid ajar until a spoon dragged across the bottom leaves a clear trail for a moment. If it thickens before the meat or vegetables are tender, add a splash of stock or water and partly cover it. For stock, keep the lid off and don't stir, so it stays clear.

**Finishing:** tilt the pot and spoon off fat that pools at the edge, or chill it and lift off the solid layer. Taste at the end and add salt, a squeeze of lemon, a splash of vinegar or fresh herbs to brighten it. If you blend it smooth, let it cool a little first (see [Blending hot liquids safely](../blending-hot-liquids/)).

**Cooling and storing:** a big pot stays warm in the middle for hours. Divide it into shallow containers and get it into the fridge within 2 hours (see [Cooling and storing leftovers](../storing-leftovers/)). Most sauces, stews and stocks also freeze well in portions.
