## Summary

This pattern is for negotiators, chapter leads and the officials who support them during the rounds. It describes how to run the negotiation on one shared, consolidated text, organise the work by chapter and keep track of what has been agreed and what it was traded for.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Merge both sides' proposals into one consolidated text, with unagreed wording in square brackets. | Everyone works on the same document, and the brackets show at a glance what is still open and who proposed what. |
| Give each chapter its own working group, and leave cross-chapter trade-offs to the chief negotiators. | Experts settle the technical detail, and the people with the full picture make the trades between chapters. |
| Apply the rule that nothing is agreed until everything is agreed. | Teams can close wording provisionally without fearing that it will be banked while their own priorities are still open. |
| Keep a record of each round, with a log of linked concessions. | Long negotiations outlast their teams, and the record stops settled points being reopened or concessions being lost. |

## Context

This pattern suits bilateral and regional agreements negotiated over many rounds, often years, with a dozen or more chapters.

It does not suit very short, narrow agreements, where an exchange of letters may be enough, or the early exploratory stage before both sides have tabled text.

There's a trade-off between progress and leverage. Closing chapters early builds momentum and reduces the workload, but a closed chapter can no longer be traded. Experienced teams close the technical chapters early and keep the commercially sensitive ones, such as market access for goods, open to the end.

## Usage

**Building the text:** one side, often the one that tabled the first proposal for a chapter, prepares the consolidated draft. Where the wording differs, both versions go in square brackets, sometimes tagged with the proposing party. "Bracketed text" means wording not yet agreed, and removing the last brackets in a chapter means it's provisionally closed. Use one master copy per chapter with strict version control, because the text circulates between capitals between rounds.

**Running the rounds:** working groups meet in parallel and report to the chief negotiators at the end of each day. Issues that one group can't settle, or that depend on another chapter, go up to the chief negotiators rather than staying bracketed indefinitely. Chief negotiators trade across chapters, guided by [knowing your walkaway](../know-your-walkaway/) and the limits in [the negotiating mandate](../trade-negotiating-mandate/).

**Keeping records:** after each round, record what moved, what was offered and what each offer was conditional on. The WTO's Doha Round treats this as a "single undertaking", in which nothing is agreed until everything is agreed. A bilateral negotiation works the same way, so the record of linked concessions is what lets you unwind a package if the other half falls through.

**Closing:** when the text is clean of brackets and leaders announce the conclusion, the work moves on to the [legal scrub and signing](../legal-scrub-and-signing/). The text can still change in wording, but not in substance.
