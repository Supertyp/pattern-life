## Summary

This pattern is for anyone putting a design onto fabric for hand embroidery. It describes how to choose a transfer method that suits the fabric, and how to keep the lines fine, accurate and removable.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| On light, thin fabric, trace the design against a light box or a sunny window. | It's quick, cheap and accurate, and needs nothing more than a marking pen or pencil. |
| On dark, thick or textured fabric, print or trace the design onto water-soluble stabiliser and stitch through it. | You can see the lines on any fabric, and the stabiliser dissolves in cool water when you're done. |
| Test any marking pen on a scrap of the same fabric, including washing and pressing it, before using it on your piece. | Some inks don't wash out of every fabric, and some come back or leave a stain. |
| Never iron over water-soluble or heat-erasable pen marks until they've been removed. | Heat can set water-soluble ink permanently, and heat-erasable ink can reappear as a pale ghost line in the cold. |
| Draw thin, light lines, and mark just inside the edge of shapes you'll fill. | Your stitches need to cover every line, and thick lines show at the edges of fine stitching. |

## Context

This pattern suits surface embroidery on cotton, linen and similar fabrics, from simple line work to filled designs.

It does not suit counted cross-stitch, where you follow a chart instead of marking the fabric (see [Counted cross-stitch basics](../cross-stitch-basics/)), or iron-on transfers that come with a kit, which have their own instructions and are usually permanent.

There's a trade-off between convenience and permanence. Erasable pens are quick, but teachers such as Mary Corbet distrust them on anything precious because marks can return or discolour. Water-soluble stabiliser and graphite or chalk pencil are slower but more predictable. This pattern suggests a water-soluble pen or pencil, tested first, for everyday pieces, and stabiliser for dark fabric or heirloom work.

## Usage

**Preparing:** wash and iron the fabric first (see [Choosing fabric, thread and needle](../embroidery-materials/)). Mark the centre of the design and the centre of the fabric so the two line up, and leave plenty of margin for the hoop. Simplify the design if you need to: you only have to mark outlines and a few guide lines, not every shading detail.

**Tracing with light:** tape the design to a light box, a window or a tablet showing a white screen, then tape the fabric over it, keeping the grain straight. Trace with a fine water-soluble pen, a sharp hard pencil or a fine chalk pencil, using light strokes. Hoop the fabric afterwards (see [Hooping fabric drum-tight](../hooping-fabric/)), or trace with the fabric in the hoop, face down, to keep it flat.

**Using stabiliser:** for dark fabric, print the design onto printable water-soluble stabiliser, or trace it with a pencil, then stick or tack it onto the fabric and hoop both together. Stitch through it. When you've finished, soak the piece in cool water for a few minutes and rinse until no stiffness remains (see [Washing, pressing and finishing a piece](../finishing-embroidery/)).

**Other methods:** dressmaker's carbon or transfer paper works on fabric you can't see through: lay it ink side down, put the design on top and trace firmly with a stylus or empty ballpoint. Choose a washable kind. Heat-transfer pencils give a permanent line, so only use them where stitching will cover every mark.
