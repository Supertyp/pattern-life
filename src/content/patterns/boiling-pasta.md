## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks pasta at home. It describes how to boil it so it's evenly cooked, properly seasoned and ready to take a sauce, and how to choose a shape that suits the sauce.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use plenty of water (about 1 litre per 100 g of pasta), bring it to a rolling boil, then salt it well. | Plenty of water returns to the boil quickly and gives the pasta room to move, and salted water is the only chance to season the pasta itself. |
| Stir in the first minute or two, and leave oil out of the water. | Pasta sticks while its surface starch is still soft. Stirring stops that, whereas oil only floats on top and later coats the pasta so the sauce slides off. |
| Start tasting about 2 minutes before the packet time and drain when it's al dente. | Packet times are a guide only, and pasta keeps softening after it's drained. |
| Scoop out a cup of the cooking water before you drain, and don't rinse the pasta. | The starchy water helps a sauce cling and loosen, and rinsing washes that starch off. The exception is pasta for a cold salad, which you rinse to stop it cooking and clumping. |
| Match the shape to the sauce: thin strands for smooth, oily or light sauces, and ribbons, tubes and shapes for chunky or meaty ones. | A light sauce coats thin strands evenly, while chunks of meat or vegetables slide off them but get caught by wide ribbons, ridges and hollows. |

## Context

This pattern suits dried durum-wheat pasta in all its shapes and fresh egg pasta, whether bought or homemade.

It does not suit pasta cooked in the sauce itself (one-pot pasta or baked pasta), gluten-free pasta, which is often better rinsed briefly, or Asian noodles, which have their own methods.

There's a trade-off between water and energy. A big pot of water takes longer to heat and uses more energy, and some cooks use less water for starchier cooking water. This pattern prefers plenty of water because it's more forgiving, but a smaller pot works if you stir more often.

## Usage

**Salting:** add the salt once the water boils, so it dissolves straight away. About a heaped teaspoon (7 to 10 g) of salt per litre makes the water taste pleasantly salty, like a well-seasoned soup. Go lighter if the sauce is salty, such as one with olives, anchovies, bacon or a lot of hard cheese.

**Getting to al dente:** put the lid on to bring the water to the boil, then take it off once the pasta goes in so it doesn't boil over. Keep the water at a lively boil. Bite a piece a couple of minutes before the packet time. Al dente means tender, with a tiny firm core but no chalky white centre. If you'll finish the pasta in the pan with its sauce, drain it a minute earlier (see [Bringing pasta and sauce together](../saucing-pasta/)).

**Choosing a shape:** spaghetti and linguine suit olive oil, garlic, seafood and smooth tomato sauces. Wide ribbons such as tagliatelle and pappardelle hold creamy or meaty sauces. Tubes and ridged shapes such as penne and rigatoni trap chunky sauces, twists such as fusilli catch pesto and finely chopped sauces, and tiny shapes belong in soup.

**Fresh versus dried:** fresh egg pasta cooks in 2 to 4 minutes, so have the sauce ready before it goes in (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)). It's soft and absorbent, which suits butter and cream sauces and rich, slow-cooked ones (see [Slow-simmering a sauce](../simmering-sauces/)). Dried pasta is firmer and stands up to oil-based and robust sauces. Neither is better, they just suit different dishes.
