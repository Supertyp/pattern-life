## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who runs or attends work meetings. It describes how to give each meeting a purpose and agenda, keep it to time, and make sure decisions and actions are recorded.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Write the purpose of the meeting in one sentence. | If you can't say why you're meeting, it may be better handled by email or a short message. |
| Send the agenda with the invitation, at least a day ahead where possible. | People can prepare, read papers and decide whether they need to attend. |
| Invite only the people who are needed for the decisions. | Smaller meetings move faster, and others can read the summary. |
| Put a time limit on each agenda item. | Timeboxing keeps the meeting focused and shows where the most important discussion belongs. |
| Start and end on time. | Waiting for latecomers penalises those who arrived on time, and running over disrupts everyone's next commitment. |
| Default to shorter meetings, such as 25 or 50 minutes instead of 30 or 60. | People get a short break before their next meeting, and shorter slots encourage focus. |
| Record decisions and actions, each with an owner and a due date. | Without a named owner, actions tend not to happen. |
| Send a short summary within a day. | Everyone, including people who didn't attend, has the same record of what was agreed. |
| Decline politely when an invitation has no purpose or agenda. | Asking for an agenda first is reasonable and often improves the meeting for everyone. |

## Context

This pattern suits team meetings, project meetings, planning sessions and decision-making meetings.

It does not suit informal social catch-ups, one-to-one check-ins that are meant to be open, or brainstorming sessions where a loose structure is the point. Even those benefit from a stated purpose and an end time.

There's a trade-off between structure and flexibility. A strict agenda may cut off a useful tangent. This pattern favours structure, but leaves a few minutes at the end for "anything else", and moves larger new topics to a separate meeting.

## Usage

**Before the meeting:** write the purpose, list agenda items with time limits, and attach any papers. Check whether email would do instead. If you're presenting an item, a quick timed run-through helps, as in [Rehearse your talk to time](../short-talk-rehearsal/).

**During the meeting:** start on time, restate the purpose and keep to the timeboxes. Note decisions and actions as you go, and read them back at the end.

**After the meeting:** send the summary, with decisions, actions, owners and due dates. Avoid scheduling meetings across others' protected time, as in [Protect blocks of focus time](../focus-blocks/), and keep follow-up emails brief, as in [Process email in batches](../inbox-batching/).

## References

Many organisations publish meeting guidelines in their staff handbooks, and professional bodies for governance and management describe similar practices for agendas and minutes.
