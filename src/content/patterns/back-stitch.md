## Summary

This pattern is for anyone learning hand embroidery. It describes back stitch, the simplest solid line stitch, and the small habits that make the line crisp and even.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep every stitch the same length, about 2 to 4 mm. | Even stitches read as a smooth, continuous line, while mixed lengths look shaky. |
| Go back down into the exact hole at the end of the previous stitch. | Stitches that share a hole meet without gaps, so the line looks unbroken. |
| Shorten the stitches on curves, to about 1.5 to 2 mm. | Short stitches follow a curve, while long ones turn it into a row of straight segments. |
| Place a stitch end exactly on every corner or point. | A stitch that bends round a corner rounds it off, while an end in the corner keeps it sharp. |
| Use two strands for fine lines and lettering, and three for bolder outlines. | Strand count sets the weight of the line, so choose it before you start and keep it consistent. |

## Context

This pattern suits outlines, lettering, stems, fine details such as whiskers and eyelashes, and the outlines in counted cross-stitch (see [Counted cross-stitch basics](../cross-stitch-basics/)).

It does not suit long, flowing curves where you want a rope-like line, for which [Stem stitch](../stem-stitch/) is smoother, or filling shapes, for which you need a fill stitch such as [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/).

There's a trade-off between the two ways of working it. In the stab method you take each stitch in two movements, straight down and straight up, with the fabric drum-tight in a hoop. In the sewing method you scoop the needle in and out in one movement, which is quicker but needs slacker fabric and gives less even stitches. This pattern uses the stab method because it gives the most control. Either is fine, and Mary Corbet notes that when you stab, the direction you work in doesn't matter.

## Usage

**Getting ready:** hoop the fabric drum-tight (see [Hooping fabric drum-tight](../hooping-fabric/)). Thread an embroidery (crewel) needle, which has a sharp point and a long eye, with two or three strands of stranded cotton (floss) about 40 to 50 cm long (see [Managing floss](../managing-thread/)). Start the thread without a knot if you can (see [Starting and ending threads neatly](../starting-and-ending-threads/)).

**Working the stitch:** back stitch moves forward on the back and backward on the front. Bring the needle up on the line one stitch length (say 3 mm) from the start of the line. Take it down at the very start of the line, making the first stitch. Bring it up again 3 mm further along the line, ahead of the first stitch. Take it back down into the hole where the needle first came up, so the two stitches share a hole. Repeat: up one stitch length ahead, down into the end of the last stitch. On the back you'll see longer, overlapping stitches, which is normal.

**Curves and corners:** shorten the stitches as the curve tightens, and judge their length by eye rather than measuring. For a corner, plan the stitches so one ends exactly at the point, then start the next direction from that hole. Pull each stitch just snug enough to lie flat; pulling hard puckers the fabric and pulls the holes apart.

**Variations:** for an even smoother, slightly raised line, whip it. Bring a second thread up at the start and slide the needle under each back stitch from the same side, without going through the fabric. Back stitch is also the base for laced and Pekinese stitch, so it's worth practising until your stitches look like a string of even beads.
