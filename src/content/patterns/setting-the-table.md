## Summary

This pattern is for anyone laying the table for an everyday meal or a relaxed dinner with guests. It describes a simple place setting, what to put in the middle of the table, and how to clear it afterwards.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Centre the plate or bowl in front of each chair, forks on the left, and knives (blade facing the plate) and spoons on the right. | It's the layout most people expect, so nobody hunts for cutlery, and an inward-facing blade is safer and tidier. |
| Lay cutlery in the order it will be used, working from the outside in. | Each course takes the outermost pieces, so guests don't have to guess. |
| Put the glass top right, above the knife, the napkin on the left or on the plate, and any bread plate top left. | Right-handed people reach for a drink without crossing the plate, and everyone knows which bread plate and glass are theirs. |
| Set only what the meal needs. | A soup spoon nobody uses is clutter, washing up and a small puzzle for your guests. |
| Put shared things in the middle before anyone sits: serving dishes with their own spoons, salt and pepper, a water jug and any condiments, such as grated cheese. | Nobody has to get up once the meal starts, and serving spoons keep personal cutlery out of the shared food. |

## Context

This pattern suits family dinners, casual meals with friends and most home entertaining.

It does not suit formal dinners with many courses, a full set of wine glasses and dessert cutlery laid above the plate. Formal settings have their own conventions, and the guides below cover them. Customs also vary between countries: in Britain the dessert spoon and fork often sit above the plate, in much of continental Europe the fork can be laid tines down, and in many parts of the world the meal isn't eaten with a knife and fork at all. Follow your household's and your guests' customs where they differ.

There's a trade-off between tradition and practicality. A napkin under the fork looks neat, but on a small table a napkin on the plate saves space. Use the version that fits your table, as long as each place is consistent.

## Usage

**Lay it in one pass:** count the chairs, then carry plates, cutlery, glasses and napkins in that order, setting all the places with one item before moving to the next. It's the [Mise en place](../mise-en-place/) idea applied to the table, and it's quicker than finishing each place in turn.

**Match the setting to the food:** a pasta dish needs a warmed bowl or a shallow plate and a fork, with a spoon only if your guests like one (see [Eating pasta neatly](../eating-pasta/)). Soup needs a soup spoon on the far right. A steak needs a sharp knife. Warm the plates or bowls in a low oven or with hot water just before serving, especially for pasta (see [Bringing pasta and sauce together](../saucing-pasta/)), which cools quickly on a cold plate.

**The middle of the table:** put serving dishes where everyone can reach them, each with its own serving spoon, and keep salt and pepper together, since people usually pass them as a pair. Pass dishes in one direction, usually to the right, so nothing arrives from both sides at once. Put a jug of water and a small bowl of grated cheese, sauce or bread out wherever the meal calls for them.

**Clearing:** wait until everyone has finished, then take plates away one or two at a time, traditionally from each person's right, rather than stacking and scraping at the table. Scrape in the kitchen and load as you go (see [Loading and unloading the dishwasher](../dishwasher-loading-unloading/)).
