## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making French knots in hand embroidery. It describes how to wrap the thread, where to take the needle down and how to hold the tension, so each knot is neat, firm and the same size as its neighbours.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Take the needle down very close to where it came up, about 1 mm away, not into the same hole. | Going down the same hole lets the knot slip straight through to the back. |
| Keep the working thread taut with your other hand until the needle is almost all the way through. | Tension holds the wraps snug on the needle; slack thread makes a loose, loopy knot. |
| Wrap once or twice, and change the number of strands to change the size. | Extra wraps make knots unstable and lumpy; more strands make a bigger knot that still sits neatly. |
| Work with the fabric drum-tight in a hoop. | Taut fabric gives the knot something firm to sit on and frees one hand to hold the thread. |
| Push the needle straight down, perpendicular to the fabric. | A slanted needle drags the wraps sideways and distorts the knot. |

## Context

This pattern suits flower centres, eyes, berries, dotted lines and textured fills made of many knots packed together. It needs both hands, so use a hoop or a stand (see [Hooping fabric drum-tight](../hooping-fabric/)).

It does not suit items that are washed hard or rubbed often, where raised knots can snag, or designs needing very large dots; [Satin stitch](../satin-stitch/) gives a flatter, tougher dot.

There's a trade-off in the number of wraps. Some teachers use three or more wraps for big knots, but most, including the Royal School of Needlework, recommend one or two, because more wraps loosen and topple. This pattern uses two wraps with two or three strands as the default; for a bigger knot add strands or switch to thicker thread rather than more wraps. Practitioners also wrap in different directions; both work if you're consistent.

## Usage

**Making a knot:** thread two strands of stranded cotton (the six-strand floss sold in skeins). A milliner or straw needle, which is the same thickness all the way along, slides through the wraps easily, but an ordinary embroidery needle works too (see [Choosing fabric, thread and needle](../embroidery-materials/)). Bring the needle up where the knot should sit. Hold the thread about 5 cm from the fabric between the thumb and finger of your non-stitching hand and pull it gently taut. Lay the needle in front of the thread and wrap the thread around the needle twice.

**Going down:** still holding the thread taut, turn the needle tip back towards the fabric and insert it about 1 mm from where it came up: a fabric thread or two away, never into the same hole. Slide the wraps down the needle so they sit on the fabric. Push the needle straight down until only the eye is above the fabric. Keep the thread taut, then pull the needle through from the back slowly, letting the thread slide through your fingers until the knot settles. Don't yank it, or the knot pulls through.

**Size and consistency:** hold the same tension and use the same number of wraps for every knot in a group. Tighter tension gives a smaller, neater knot; slightly looser gives a softer, larger one.

**Moving between knots:** on light fabric, don't carry the thread more than about 1 cm across the back between knots, because it shows through. End off and restart instead (see [Starting and ending threads neatly](../starting-and-ending-threads/) and [Planning a design](../planning-stitch-order/)). A knot makes a natural centre for a [Lazy daisy](../lazy-daisy-stitch/) flower.
